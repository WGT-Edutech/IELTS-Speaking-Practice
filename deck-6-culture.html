Culture & Tradition

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Art

Do you like art?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I do, though my taste is admittedly broad rather than expert. I'm drawn to modern art — particularly pieces that challenge the way you see the everyday — but I've also grown to appreciate classical painting in recent years, largely because I took the trouble to learn what to look for. What I find most compelling about art is its capacity to express something that language can't quite capture, which is probably why it has survived in every culture in every era.

Question 2 · Food

What kinds of food are popular in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The food landscape in my country is wonderfully diverse, though a few staples define it. We're known for hearty, home-cooked dishes built on fresh local ingredients, and there's a strong tradition of family recipes passed down through the generations. That said, the cuisine has become genuinely cosmopolitan — you'll find excellent restaurants serving food from virtually every corner of the world in the larger cities, and younger people in particular embrace that fusion enthusiastically.

Question 3 · Museums

Are museums popular in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

They're popular, though I suspect they attract a specific audience rather than universal appeal. School groups visit regularly, and there's a solid core of cultural enthusiasts who never miss a major exhibition, but I'd guess a significant portion of the population rarely sets foot in one. What's encouraging is that museums have reinvented themselves — interactive displays, evening events, and social media presence have made them far more accessible than the dusty institutions they once were, and attendance is rising as a result.

Question 4 · Flowers

Is it popular to give flowers to people in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Yes, it's a deeply rooted custom, though the etiquette around it is quite specific. Flowers are given for celebrations — birthdays, anniversaries, hospital visits — and there's a whole unspoken language around which flowers suit which occasion, with certain blooms reserved for particular meanings. What's interesting is that the tradition is holding up well despite the rise of digital alternatives, which I think reflects the simple fact that receiving real flowers is a genuinely personal gesture that no emoji can replicate.

Question 5 · Festival

What is the most important festival in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Without question, our new year festival is the most important, and it dominates the cultural calendar for weeks. Families travel huge distances to be together, the streets fill with elaborate decorations, and there's a wonderful sense of collective renewal — settling old debts, cleaning the home, and beginning the year with good intentions. What moves me about it is the way it binds generations: the rituals are performed with the same care by grandparents and children alike, which gives the festival a continuity that feels almost sacred.

Question 6 · Clothes

Are there any traditional clothes in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

There are, though they're worn primarily on ceremonial occasions rather than in daily life. The traditional dress is elaborate and regional, with distinct styles and colours that vary by area, and it's typically reserved for weddings, festivals, and formal celebrations. I find it genuinely beautiful, and I'm glad the tradition persists, though I'm also aware it's at risk — fewer young people know how to make it, and it's increasingly viewed as costume rather than clothing. So I'd say it survives, but its future depends on whether it's kept alive as living culture rather than museum piece.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Talk about a traditional product which is very popular in your country.
  • what the product is
  • how it is made
  • why it is popular
  • what it is used for
  • and say if it is popular in other countries
Band 9 · Model Answer

The traditional product I'd like to talk about is the hand-woven textile that my region is famous for, and it's a product with a great deal of history behind it.

What it is, essentially, is a type of cloth woven on traditional wooden looms, using patterns and techniques that have been passed down through families for centuries. Each piece is entirely handmade, and the patterns are distinctive — geometric motifs that carry symbolic meanings, with colours derived from natural dyes made from plants and minerals. No two pieces are identical, which is precisely the point; the subtle variations are part of the product's character and proof of its authenticity.

How it's made is almost as important as the product itself. The process begins with the preparation of the yarn, which is spun and dyed by hand, and then the weaving itself, which is slow, meticulous work — a single piece can take several weeks to complete. It's genuinely skilled craftsmanship: the weavers learn their trade over many years, and the best of them work without patterns, carrying the designs in their heads as their ancestors did.

Its popularity rests on a combination of beauty, heritage, and authenticity. People buy it because it's genuinely beautiful, of course, but also because it carries a story — it represents a connection to regional identity that mass-produced goods simply can't offer. It has also become a marker of ethical consumption, because buyers increasingly value knowing exactly who made something and how.

As for what it's used for, it depends on the pattern and the region, but the most common use is in traditional clothing — shawls, sashes, and ceremonial garments worn at weddings and festivals. It's also used in the home, as cushions, wall hangings, and table coverings, and in recent years designers have started incorporating it into contemporary fashion. Because the cloth is so durable, it's often treated as something to be kept and passed down rather than replaced, so a piece woven by one generation frequently becomes an heirloom for the next.

As for whether it's popular in other countries, increasingly yes. There's been real international interest, and the product has appeared in design exhibitions abroad and attracted a following among collectors and interior designers. What heartens me most is that this global demand has created genuine economic opportunity for the weavers themselves, helping to ensure that a craft which could easily have died out is instead thriving.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

What different types of traditional products are produced in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The range is remarkable, and I'd say it's one of the quiet strengths of the country. We produce traditional textiles and ceramics, each region with its own patterns and techniques that have been refined over centuries, alongside food products that are protected by geographic origin. There's also a rich tradition of handicrafts — woodwork, metalwork, and decorative arts that demand skills passed down through apprenticeships. What unites them is that they encode history in physical form, which is why losing a craft feels like losing a chapter of the national story.

Discussion 2

Why do traditional products attract tourists?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think traditional products attract tourists for reasons that go beyond simple souvenir-buying. There's an authenticity to them that mass-produced goods can't replicate — each piece carries the mark of a human hand and a cultural lineage, which appeals to travellers seeking something genuine. There's also an element of scarcity and story: a ceramic made in the town where the technique originated has a narrative that a factory product lacks entirely. In a world of relentless homogeneity, I suspect these products will only become more prized, precisely because they're irreplaceable.

Discussion 3

Do you think the government should help promote traditional products?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I believe the government has a legitimate role, though it should be exercised carefully. Traditional crafts face genuine market failure — skilled artisans struggle to compete with cheap imports, and the knowledge itself is fragile, so some form of support is justified on cultural heritage grounds alone. That said, I'd be wary of heavy-handed subsidies that prop up industries artificially; the more effective approach is probably indirect — promoting them abroad, funding apprenticeships, and protecting the intellectual property that local producers hold. The goal should be to make tradition self-sustaining, not museum-dependent.

Discussion 4

Do you think it is necessary to protect traditions?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Yes, I think it's necessary, though I'd distinguish between protecting traditions and freezing them in amber. Traditions provide continuity, identity, and a sense of belonging, which are genuinely valuable in a rapidly changing world, and losing them is a form of cultural impoverishment that's easy to underestimate. At the same time, traditions that can't adapt slowly suffocate — the healthiest ones evolve while retaining their core. So I'd frame protection not as resistance to change, but as stewardship: keeping the roots alive while allowing the branches to grow.
