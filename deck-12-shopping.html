Shopping & Consumer Habits

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Shopping

What do you usually shop for?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I shop for the essentials most of the time — groceries, household items, and the occasional replacement of things that have worn out. I'm not someone who derives pleasure from shopping for its own sake; I tend to treat it as an errand to be completed efficiently, which I suspect puts me in the minority. That said, I do allow myself exceptions, particularly for books and the occasional quality item of clothing, where I'm willing to spend time and money because I genuinely value what I'm buying.

Question 2 · Malls

Do you often go to malls?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Not often, and I suspect that's increasingly typical. Malls have a particular atmosphere that I find slightly exhausting — the crowds, the ambient noise, the sheer scale of choice — and I generally visit only when there's a specific purpose, like a cinema trip or a shop that exists nowhere else. What's interesting is that my behaviour has shifted almost entirely online for routine purchases, leaving the mall as a destination for experiences rather than errands. I do wonder, though, whether the pendulum will swing back, because there's something tangible and immediate about physical shopping that screens can't replicate.

Question 3 · Online

Do you shop online a lot?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I do, and I'd say the majority of my discretionary spending now happens online. The convenience is undeniable — no travel, no queues, effortless price comparison, and delivery to the door — and for standard items the experience is simply better than its physical equivalent. Where I remain cautious is with anything where fit, quality, or feel matters, because photographs can flatter and descriptions can mislead. So I've developed a pragmatic division: online for the routine, physical for the sensory.

Part 1 · Questions 4–6

Question 4 · Shopping Centres

How common are shopping centres in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

They're extremely common, to the point where most towns of any size have at least one, and the larger cities have several competing centres. The development accelerated dramatically over the past couple of decades, transforming the retail landscape and often drawing life away from traditional high streets in the process. What's notable now is the beginning of a reversal — some centres are repurposing empty retail space into leisure, dining, and residential uses, which I think reflects a genuine change in how we shop and socialise.

Question 5 · Advertising

Will you buy something because of an advertisement?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Rarely, and I'd say I'm fairly resistant to advertising as a driver of purchases. I'm aware of the techniques — urgency, social proof, aspirational imagery — and that awareness acts as a kind of immunity; I've learned to separate the appeal of the advertisement from the merit of the product. That said, I wouldn't claim total resistance: advertising shapes preferences slowly and subtly, particularly for brands and categories I don't think about critically. So my honest answer is that ads rarely trigger my purchases directly, but they probably influence them more than I'd like to admit.

Question 6 · Advertising

What makes a good advertisement?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think the best advertisements work by being useful or entertaining rather than merely loud. The ones that stick with me either tell a genuine story, make me laugh, or communicate a clear benefit in a memorable way — and crucially, they respect the viewer's intelligence, because nothing destroys trust faster than an ad that treats you as gullible. Beyond creativity, I'd argue the true test of a good advertisement is honesty: the best campaigns promise exactly what the product delivers. Everything else is decoration.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe a shop that you like to visit.
  • what things you can buy there
  • what the most popular shops are
  • what the advantages of shopping there are
  • when you usually go there
  • and explain why you enjoy going there
Band 9 · Model Answer

The shop I'd like to describe is a small independent bookshop in the centre of my city, and it has become something of a refuge for me.

What you can buy there is, of course, books — but that description undersells it considerably. The stock is carefully curated rather than simply extensive: the owner clearly thinks hard about every shelf, so the selection has a personality that chain stores completely lack. Beyond the books themselves, the shop sells a small range of related things — notebooks, cards, and literary gifts — and, importantly, it hosts readings and small events that have made it a genuine community hub rather than just a point of sale.

The most popular aspect of the shop, I think, is the atmosphere. It's the opposite of the bright, crowded chain model: it's a calm, slightly dim space with comfortable chairs, where browsing is encouraged and no one hurries you. There's a wall where staff recommend their favourite books with handwritten notes, and those personal touches — in an increasingly automated world — are exactly what draws people back.

The advantages of shopping there are substantial. You're supporting an independent business, which matters to me, and the staff genuinely know their stock, so the recommendations are excellent — I've discovered far more good books there than through any algorithm. There's also the pleasure of serendipity: you walk in for one thing and leave with three, because physical browsing offers discoveries that targeted suggestions can't replicate.

I tend to go there on weekend mornings, usually on a Saturday, when the city centre is busy but the shop itself stays calm. That's become a small ritual for me: a walk in, a coffee nearby, and then an unhurried hour browsing the shelves. It's also where I go when I need a present, because the staff are so good at recommending something for a particular person that I've more or less given up choosing gifts anywhere else. Those Saturday visits are one of the few parts of my week that I genuinely protect.

And why I enjoy going there is simple: it's one of the few places where shopping genuinely feels enjoyable rather than transactional. I go in for ten minutes and stay for an hour. It's a place that treats books as something more than products, and in doing so, it's become far more than a shop to me — it's the kind of place that reminds you why physical, local commerce still matters.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

Is shopping a popular activity in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Yes, it's a hugely popular activity, though its character has changed significantly. For a large segment of the population, shopping functions as leisure rather than necessity — browsing, comparing, and buying is treated as entertainment, and the weekend shopping trip is a genuine cultural ritual. I think that reflects both material prosperity and the social dimension of shopping, since much of it is done in company. What's changing, of course, is where and how it happens, but the underlying appetite seems undiminished.

Discussion 2

How have shopping habits changed over recent years?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The changes have been profound and remarkably rapid. The most visible shift is online: a substantial share of purchases now happens through screens, reshaping the economics of retail and leaving physical stores to adapt or close. Alongside that, the model has moved toward convenience and experience — faster delivery, easier returns, and physical stores that increasingly sell experiences rather than just goods. There's also been a cultural shift in values: younger consumers care more about sustainability, ethical sourcing, and brand authenticity than previous generations did. I think we're still in the middle of this transformation, and the final shape of shopping is far from settled.

Discussion 3

To what extent do you think advertising affects the way people shop?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'd say the influence is considerable, though it operates more subtly than people often assume. Direct effects are easy to see — advertising does drive some purchases, particularly for impulse categories — but the deeper influence is in shaping preferences, norms, and perceived needs over time, which is far harder to resist because it's largely invisible. What amplifies this is the targeting revolution: modern advertising is personalised to a degree that would have seemed like science fiction a generation ago, which makes it more effective and more concerning. My honest conclusion is that advertising shapes how we shop more than we think, and the people who recognise that are better equipped to resist it.

Discussion 4

Do you think shopping habits are likely to change in the future?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think shopping habits will continue to evolve, and I'd expect the trends already visible to deepen. The move toward online shopping will likely continue, though I suspect a stable equilibrium will emerge rather than the complete extinction of physical retail, because the experiential and sensory dimensions of shopping retain genuine appeal. I'd also predict growing emphasis on sustainability and ethics, driven by generational values, and a continuing shift from ownership toward access — renting, sharing, and subscription models for things we once bought outright. The most significant unknown is technology: augmented and virtual reality could transform online shopping into something far more immersive, and if they do, the boundaries between online and physical may dissolve entirely.
