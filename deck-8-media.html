Media & Communication

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · TV

How often do you watch TV?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Far less than I used to, which surprises even me. I'd say I watch perhaps an hour most evenings, but it's nearly always something I've chosen deliberately rather than a broadcast schedule, and I rarely watch live television anymore. The shift happened gradually — streaming made me selective, and now the idea of sitting through commercial breaks feels almost quaint. I suspect I watch more video content than ever overall, but almost none of it through a traditional television set.

Question 2 · Newspapers

Do you prefer to read local news or international news?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I gravitate toward international news, and I think that reflects how I see the world. Local news is valuable for practical matters — weather, transport, community issues — and I do keep an eye on it, but my genuine curiosity lies in what's happening globally, because the forces shaping my life are increasingly international. What I've learned, though, is that both matter and they're more connected than they appear: local events ripple outward, and global trends land locally. So I suppose my honest answer is that I read international news for understanding and local news for living.

Question 3 · Mobile Phones

At what age did you first get a mobile phone?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I got my first phone later than most of my peers — I was around fourteen, and it was a deliberate decision by my parents to delay it as long as possible. At the time I was mortified, because everyone else seemed to have one, but I now recognise the wisdom of the decision, and I'd probably make the same call as a parent. What strikes me is how different that phone was from today's devices: it had essentially one function, and I used it exactly as intended, which now seems almost charmingly simple.

Question 4 · Email

Do you prefer to email, phone, or text your friends?

Band 9 · Model Answer

It depends on the context, which I think is the only sensible answer. For quick logistics — changing a plan, confirming a time — text is perfect, because it's asynchronous and doesn't demand immediate attention. For substantive conversations, I prefer calling, because so much of communication is tone and nuance that text simply can't carry, and I've seen too many misunderstandings born of a poorly phrased message. Email, meanwhile, I reserve for things that need a record or require formality. So my choice is governed by the message rather than any fixed preference.

Question 5 · Advertisements

Do you like watching advertisements?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Generally no, and I suspect I'm not alone in that. Most advertising is designed to interrupt, and I've developed an almost automatic instinct to skip, mute, or scroll past it, which I imagine is true for most people raised on streaming and ad-blockers. That said, I can think of exceptions — the rare campaign that's genuinely witty or beautiful enough to watch voluntarily, and I'll even share those with friends. So I wouldn't say I dislike advertising per se; I dislike bad advertising, which unfortunately is the majority.

Question 6 · TV

What kind of TV programmes do you like to watch?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'm drawn to programmes that engage both the mind and the heart, which I suppose is a broad taste. Documentaries are my default — I find the natural history ones genuinely awe-inspiring, and a well-made current affairs programme can hold my attention more than any drama. That said, I do enjoy a well-crafted drama series, particularly ones with morally complex characters, and I've recently rediscovered the pleasure of comedy that's genuinely funny rather than merely loud. The common thread, I think, is that I want television to leave me with something to think about.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe an interesting piece of news that you have recently read or heard about.
  • what the story was
  • who was involved in the story
  • where you read or heard about the story
  • what happened afterwards
  • and explain why this news story was interesting to you
Band 9 · Model Answer

The news story I'd like to talk about is one I came across a few months ago, and it has stayed with me ever since.

The story concerned a significant breakthrough in renewable energy — a research group that had developed a new type of solar cell with dramatically improved efficiency and, crucially, a much lower production cost. The technical detail that captured my imagination was the claim that the new cells could be manufactured using processes similar to printing, which would make large-scale production far cheaper and faster than anything currently available.

The people involved were, in essence, an international team of scientists and engineers — researchers from several universities and a private company that had invested in commercialising the technology. What made the story compelling to me was that it wasn't a lone genius in a garage; it was a genuinely collaborative effort spanning countries, which I found a hopeful illustration of what international cooperation can achieve.

I heard about the story through a combination of sources: I first saw a summary on a science news website, then read a more detailed article in a reputable newspaper, and I even watched an interview with one of the lead researchers. What impressed me was the careful way the coverage handled the claims — the scientists were admirably honest about the gap between laboratory success and commercial deployment, which made the story feel trustworthy in a way that breathless science reporting often isn't.

As for what happened afterwards, the story didn't simply fade away. The research group secured further funding, and there were reports that a pilot production line was being developed with a manufacturing partner. The researchers, to their credit, kept tempering expectations — they spoke openly about how long it usually takes to move from a laboratory result to a product on the market. I've followed it occasionally since, and although nothing has reached consumers yet, I've been struck by how the conversation around it has stayed unusually measured and careful rather than exaggerated.

And that, I think, is why the story was so interesting to me. It wasn't just the technology itself, though that was exciting enough. It was the whole picture: the collaboration, the honest communication of uncertainty, and the sense that the story represented real, incremental progress on one of the defining challenges of our age. In a media environment dominated by conflict and crisis, a story about people genuinely working to solve a problem felt almost revolutionary, and it restored a little of my faith in both science and journalism.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

How do people get their news in today's society?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The answer has changed dramatically in the space of a single generation. The traditional model — a few broadcasters and newspapers shaping the national conversation — has fragmented into an ecosystem where people assemble their news from social media feeds, messaging apps, newsletters, and a dizzying array of digital outlets. What's gained is choice and immediacy; what's at risk is reliability, because the old editorial gatekeepers have been replaced by algorithms optimised for engagement rather than accuracy. I think the honest conclusion is that people have never had more access to news, but they've also never had more responsibility for verifying it.

Discussion 2

How has social media changed how we consume news?

Band 9 · Model Answer

It has fundamentally altered both the delivery and the trust dynamics of news. The most visible change is speed — social media breaks stories in real time, often before journalists have verified them, which has created a permanent tension between immediacy and accuracy. More subtly, social media has changed the business model, pushing outlets toward sensationalism because engagement metrics reward outrage and novelty over depth. There's also a worrying echo-chamber effect, where algorithms show people more of what they already believe, which I think has done genuine damage to shared understanding. So my verdict is double-edged: social media has democratised news but eroded its quality and trustworthiness simultaneously.

Discussion 3

Do you believe everything you read in the newspaper?

Band 9 · Model Answer

No, and I'd argue anyone who does is being naive, though the failure is more subtle than simple fabrication. The real issue isn't usually that newspapers invent stories; it's that they make editorial choices — what to cover, how to frame it, which voices to quote — that inevitably reflect a perspective. I read newspapers with that awareness, treating them as one source among several rather than a definitive account, and I find cross-referencing stories across outlets is the most reliable way to approach the truth. The danger isn't believing one lie; it's absorbing a steady drip of a single perspective without realising it.

Discussion 4

What is the difference between new media and old media?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The distinction has blurred so thoroughly that I sometimes wonder if it's still meaningful. The old media were defined by scarcity and gatekeeping — limited channels, professional producers, and a one-way flow from creator to audience. New media are defined by abundance and participation: anyone can publish, and the audience is also the producer. But the most interesting development is convergence — traditional outlets now behave like digital natives, and digital platforms have become as powerful as the old conglomerates. So the real difference isn't technology but control: new media distributed the power to publish, and we're still absorbing the consequences of that redistribution.
