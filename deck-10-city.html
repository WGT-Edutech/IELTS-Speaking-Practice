City & Urban Life

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Hometown

Where is your hometown?

Band 9 · Model Answer

My hometown is a mid-sized city in the south of the country, about two hours from the capital. It's not the kind of place that features on postcards, but it has a character I've grown to appreciate more with distance — a historic centre, a slow-moving river, and a community where people still know their neighbours. I think there's a particular affection one holds for the place where you grew up, because it shaped you in ways you only recognise in hindsight.

Question 2 · Home

Who do you live with?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I currently live with my family, which is a choice as much as a circumstance. We share the practical responsibilities of the household, and there's a comfortable rhythm to it that I value, though I'll admit the lack of privacy occasionally tests my patience. I suspect that in a few years I'll want my own space, but for now the arrangement works well, and it's certainly helped me save money in a way that wouldn't have been possible living alone.

Question 3 · Accommodation

Do you live in a house or a flat?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I live in a flat, and I've spent most of my life in flats, so it feels entirely natural to me. It's in a purpose-built block with a decent amount of natural light, and while the space is compact, I've learned to use it efficiently, which I think is a genuine skill in its own right. I sometimes fantasise about a house with a garden, but I'm also realistic: the convenience of city living, with everything within reach, more than compensates for the extra square metres I'd gain elsewhere.

Question 4 · Transport

What kinds of public transport do you have in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

We have a reasonably comprehensive network, though its quality varies by region. The larger cities boast an extensive bus and metro system that's genuinely efficient, and intercity rail connects the major hubs well. What's less impressive is the coverage in rural areas, where services are sparse and frequency is poor, which leaves many people dependent on cars. My honest assessment is that the system works well where demand is high and struggles where it isn't, which I suppose is the economics of public transport in a nutshell.

Question 5 · Neighbours

Do you know the people who live next door to you?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I do, at least by name, though the depth of the relationships varies considerably. On my floor I know the immediate neighbours reasonably well — we've helped each other with packages and the occasional emergency, and there's a friendly familiarity that makes the building feel less anonymous. Beyond that, the connections thin out, which I think reflects the reality of modern urban living: we're friendly, but we're not close. I do value what exists, because a building where people acknowledge each other is a very different place to live.

Question 6 · Hometown

Is there any way your hometown could be made better?

Band 9 · Model Answer

There are a few changes I'd love to see, and they're mostly about liveability rather than grand ambition. I'd prioritise more green space, because the concrete-heavy areas of town become genuinely oppressive in summer, and I'd invest heavily in public transport to reduce the car dependency that clogs the streets at rush hour. I'd also like to see more support for the independent shops and cafes that give the town its character, since they're increasingly squeezed by rents and competition from chains. None of these are revolutionary, but collectively they'd transform the daily experience.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe your favourite city or a city you have visited that you like very much.
  • what its name is and where it is
  • when you visited it
  • why you liked it
  • how long you stayed there
  • and describe the major attractions in this city
Band 9 · Model Answer

The city I'd like to describe is one I visited a few years ago on holiday, and it made an impression on me that has genuinely lasted.

It's a historic port city in southern Europe, built on a series of hills that rise steeply from the sea. I visited during early autumn, which I suspect is the perfect time to see it — the summer crowds had thinned, but the weather was still warm and generous.

Why I liked it so much is difficult to isolate to a single reason, because the appeal was cumulative. The city had a scale that felt human — walkable, with each turn revealing something unexpected: a tiny square, a hidden church, a view that stopped you in your tracks. There was also a palpable sense of history; the streets had been walked for centuries, and the buildings carried the marks of that depth in a way that made me feel I was moving through living layers of time rather than a preserved museum.

The major attractions were genuinely remarkable. The old town was dominated by a cathedral that took centuries to complete, and its architecture showed exactly that — styles from different eras layered into one building, which I found more beautiful than any perfectly consistent structure. There was also a fortress overlooking the harbour, and I remember standing on its ramparts watching the ferries come and go, imagining all the journeys that had ended or begun there. And the food — the city was famous for its seafood, and the restaurants along the waterfront served dishes that I still think about, which I suppose is as strong a recommendation as any.

I stayed for about five days, which turned out to be almost the perfect amount of time. It was long enough to stop feeling like a tourist and start falling into a routine — the same café in the morning, the same walk along the harbour in the evening — but short enough that I never tired of it. In hindsight, that stay struck a balance I've rarely managed since: long enough to see the city properly, but short enough to leave wanting more.

What I remember most, though, wasn't any single attraction but the overall atmosphere — a city that felt confident in its history and entirely comfortable in itself. I've visited grander cities, but none that made me feel, within days, that I could happily live there. That, I think, is the mark of a truly great city.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

In your opinion, what makes a city a good one to live in?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think a good city balances three things that are in constant tension: opportunity, comfort, and community. Opportunity means jobs, education, and the chance to advance; comfort means safety, affordable housing, and functioning services; and community means the human texture that makes a place feel like home rather than a collection of buildings. The cities that succeed, in my view, are those that treat these as connected rather than competing priorities — walkable neighbourhoods, reliable transport, and public spaces where people actually want to gather. The test of a good city, ultimately, is whether it works well for the people who live there every day, not just for visitors.

Discussion 2

What are the advantages of living in a city?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The advantages are substantial, and I think they boil down to density. Cities concentrate opportunity — jobs, education, culture, and services all sit within reach, which creates a self-reinforcing dynamism that's hard to replicate elsewhere. There's also an intangible energy to urban life: the proximity of so many people, ideas, and experiences makes cities stimulating in a way that can be genuinely addictive. And practically, cities tend to be more efficient — better transport, better healthcare, better access to everything from groceries to entertainment. The trade-off is real, but for many people the calculus clearly favours the city.

Discussion 3

What are the negative aspects of crowded cities?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Crowding exacts a genuine toll, and I'd identify three costs that are easy to overlook. The first is temporal: congestion in cities doesn't just waste time in traffic — it inflates the cost of everything, because time and transport are embedded in every price. The second is psychological: constant density and noise create a low-grade stress that people absorb without noticing, and I suspect it contributes to the mental health pressures associated with urban living. The third is spatial: crowded cities price ordinary people out of decent housing, which forces long commutes and undermines the very communities that make city life rewarding. So the challenge of crowded cities isn't crowding itself — it's managing density so its benefits outweigh its costs.

Discussion 4

How can governments improve living standards in crowded cities?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think the most effective interventions address the root causes rather than the symptoms. Housing affordability is central, because it drives the commutes, the crowding, and the inequality that undermine living standards — so policies that expand the supply of genuinely affordable housing would ripple through everything else. Alongside that, investment in public transport is transformative, because it reduces congestion and reconnects people to opportunity. I'd also advocate for a focus on public space and green infrastructure, which measurably improves wellbeing and cohesion. None of these are quick fixes, but they're the levers that actually move the needle.
