IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Computer

Do you use computers?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Constantly, to the point where I'd struggle to separate my working life from them. I use them for communication, research, document creation, and data analysis — essentially every professional task I perform flows through a computer in some form. The interesting shift is that I've stopped thinking of them as tools and started treating them as infrastructure, in the same way I don't think about electricity. That said, I'm old enough to remember a world without them, which gives me a slightly different perspective from those who've never known otherwise.

Question 2 · Internet

What are your favourite websites?

Band 9 · Model Answer

My favourites are the ones that make me feel like I've gained something — genuine knowledge, a new skill, or a fresh perspective. I spend a lot of time on reference sites and educational platforms, and I have a weakness for long-form journalism that treats a complex subject properly. Beyond that, I use a handful of utilitarian sites daily — banking, transport, and the like — though those feel more like obligations than favourites. What unites the sites I love is that they respect my attention rather than harvesting it.

Question 3 · Mobile Phones

What do you most use your mobile phone for?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Mostly practical things, though I suspect that's true for nearly everyone. Communication dominates — messaging, calls, and the occasional video chat — but I'm also heavily reliant on it for navigation, payments, and photography, which I'd have found astonishing fifteen years ago. What's shifted most is the device's role as a capture tool: I take more photos and notes on my phone than anywhere else, which means it's quietly become my external memory. I do try to moderate my use, but the honest truth is that the phone has absorbed functions I never anticipated.

Question 4 · Books

Do you think electronic books are better than real books?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I keep coming back to the same conclusion: it depends entirely on the purpose. For reference, search, and study, electronic books are genuinely superior — instant access, searchable text, and a library that fits in your pocket. But for immersive reading, I still prefer paper, and I'm not sure that's merely nostalgia; there's evidence that physical books support deeper comprehension and better spatial memory of the text. So I've ended up with both: a device for utility and a shelf for pleasure, and I see no reason to choose between them.

Question 5 · Science

Did you enjoy science classes at school?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I did, though my enthusiasm was uneven across subjects. The subjects that engaged me were the ones that connected to real things — biology, because it explained the living world around me, and practical experiments, because they made abstract ideas tangible. I was considerably less enamoured with the more abstract theory, largely because I couldn't see its relevance at the time. In hindsight, I recognise that the best teachers were the ones who showed the why behind the what, which is a lesson I've carried into how I approach learning as an adult.

Question 6 · Internet

How will the Internet develop in the future?

Band 9 · Model Answer

If the past two decades are any guide, the Internet will develop in directions that are genuinely hard to anticipate, but I can sketch a few trends. I expect it to become more embedded — moving off screens and into infrastructure, with connectivity woven into everything from vehicles to appliances. I also suspect it will become more intelligent in the delivery sense, with the crude search paradigm giving way to more proactive and personalised assistance. The question that worries me is governance: the Internet's future will be shaped less by technology than by the political choices we make about privacy, openness, and control, and that's a conversation we're only beginning to have properly.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe an area of science that you are interested in.
  • what it is
  • what you can learn from it
  • when you started becoming interested in it
  • how you keep up with the subject
  • and explain why you find it interesting
Band 9 · Model Answer

The area of science that fascinates me is neuroscience — the study of the brain and the nervous system — and I've been captivated by it for longer than I can easily account for.

What it is, in essence, is the attempt to understand the most complex object in the known universe: the three-pound organ that produces consciousness, memory, emotion, and every decision we've ever made. The field spans an extraordinary range, from the molecular level — how individual neurons communicate — to the systems level, such as how networks of brain regions give rise to behaviour and identity.

What you can learn from it is almost boundless, and that's part of its appeal. You can learn how memories are formed and why they fade, how the brain rewires itself through experience, how sleep consolidates learning, and how conditions like depression or addiction alter neural circuits. For me, the most profound lessons are philosophical as much as scientific — studying the brain confronts you with questions about free will and the nature of the self, but now with tools to investigate them empirically.

When I started becoming interested in it is a little difficult to pin down, but I can identify the moment it crystallised. It was during a psychology class in school, when the teacher explained how a patient with damage to a specific brain region lost the ability to recognise faces while everything else remained intact. The idea that a particular aspect of experience could be localised to a particular piece of tissue struck me as extraordinary, and I've been reading about the subject ever since.

I keep up with it mostly through reading and listening rather than formal study. I follow a handful of science communicators and researchers, I listen to a few long-form neuroscience podcasts on my commute, and I read popular books whenever a particularly good one arrives. I'm also careful to check the original research rather than relying on press releases, because the gap between a headline and an actual finding can be enormous. It's a slow way to learn, but it means I'm never short of something interesting to dig into.

And I find it endlessly interesting because it sits at the frontier of so many questions that matter. Understanding the brain promises better treatments for devastating conditions, more effective education, and deeper insight into what makes us who we are. Every answer neuroscience provides seems to open three new questions, and I find that inexhaustible — it's a field where the more you learn, the more awe you feel.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

Do you think science is important?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Yes, I think science is important in a way that almost defies adequate expression. It's the most reliable method humanity has devised for distinguishing what's true from what merely feels true, and that discipline of evidence underlies everything from medicine to engineering to the devices we use daily. What's more, science is fundamentally self-correcting — it's built to revise its own conclusions, which is exactly the opposite of dogma. My concern is that scientific thinking is often treated as a niche interest rather than a basic civic skill, and I think a society that undervalues evidence will make worse decisions in every domain.

Discussion 2

Which area of science has been the most important in the last hundred years?

Band 9 · Model Answer

If I had to pick one, I'd nominate the discovery of antibiotics, because its impact on human life is almost impossible to overstate. Before penicillin, a minor infection could be a death sentence, and surgery was far riskier than it is today; antibiotics turned that world on its head and added decades to average lifespans. That said, the case for computing and the Internet is compelling too, since they've transformed how we live, work, and think on a global scale. What's striking is that these breakthroughs compound — antibiotics kept populations healthy enough to build the technological age, which shows how scientific advances reinforce each other.

Discussion 3

Why do you think some inventions have not been successful?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Failure is far more common than success, and I think the reasons are instructive. Many inventions fail for economic reasons rather than technical ones — they work perfectly but nobody needs them, or they arrive before the infrastructure and ecosystem that would make them viable exists. Others fail because of what I'd call the marketing gap: brilliant technology presented so poorly that no one understands what it's for. And some simply lose the race to a superior competitor, which is the brutal reality of innovation. The pattern I've noticed is that successful inventions usually solve a genuine, felt problem at the right moment, and timing is the factor that's hardest to control.

Discussion 4

Do you think there will be any negative effects from future technology?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'm certain there will be negative effects, because every transformative technology in history has come with unintended consequences, and I see no reason to expect an exception. Automation and artificial intelligence, for example, promise enormous benefits, but they also threaten significant disruption to employment and could concentrate power in worrying ways if not governed thoughtfully. My concern is less about the technology itself than our track record of managing its side effects — we tend to adopt first and regulate later, often when the damage is already done. That said, I'm not pessimistic; I'm realistic. The goal shouldn't be to avoid downsides entirely, which is impossible, but to build the institutions that can respond to them.
