Technology & Society

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

How to Ace This

General

Only four things affect your score: Fluency, Vocabulary, Grammar, Pronunciation. Not body language. Not eye contact. Not smiling.

Part 1 Tip

Give 2-3 sentence answers. Never just say yes or no. If you do not understand a word, ask: What do you mean by...?

Part 2 Tip

The bullet points are hints, not requirements. Focus on the main topic. If stuck, add: how you felt, what others thought, or compare to the past.

Tech Topic

When describing technology, do not just list features. Explain how it changed your daily life. Personal impact impresses examiners more than technical specs.

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Internet

How essential is the Internet to your daily routine?

Band 9 · Model Answer

To be perfectly honest, the Internet has become so integral to my daily routine that I'd struggle to function without it. From the moment I wake up, I rely on it for everything — checking the weather, reading the news, messaging colleagues, even ordering my groceries. It's not simply a convenience anymore; it's woven into the fabric of my day. That said, I do try to carve out screen-free periods in the evening, because I've noticed that constant connectivity, for all its benefits, can leave you feeling perpetually switched on.

Question 2 · Internet

Would you say you are reliant on digital devices?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Absolutely, and I think that's true of virtually everyone my age. My phone has essentially become an extension of myself — I use it for navigation, banking, photography, and staying in touch with family abroad, so when I leave it at home, I genuinely feel a sense of disorientation. I'm not particularly proud of that dependency, and I've made a conscious effort to dial it back, but I'd be deluding myself if I claimed I could live without these devices entirely.

Question 3 · Mobile Phones

What do you predominantly use your mobile phone for?

Band 9 · Model Answer

These days it's overwhelmingly for communication and organisation rather than anything glamorous. Messaging apps dominate, particularly for coordinating with family and friends, but I also lean on it heavily for calendars, to-do lists, and the occasional video call. Interestingly, I've noticed I make far fewer actual voice calls than I did a decade ago — it's almost as if the phone has transformed from a device for talking into a pocket-sized command centre for my entire life.

Part 1 · Questions 4–6

Question 4 · Computer

Do you think computers will eventually obsolete traditional forms of work?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I don't believe computers will wipe out traditional work altogether, but they will unquestionably reshape it beyond recognition. Automation is already absorbing repetitive tasks in manufacturing, accounting, and even aspects of legal work, and that trajectory is unlikely to reverse. However, roles that demand genuine human judgement — counselling, creative direction, skilled trades — will probably prove remarkably resilient, precisely because they rely on empathy and intuition, which machines struggle to replicate. So I'd frame it less as obsolescence and more as a profound redistribution of what we do.

Question 5 · Email

Do you anticipate email replacing all other forms of correspondence?

Band 9 · Model Answer

No, I'd argue the opposite is happening. Email is steadily being displaced by instant messaging, even in professional settings, because people crave immediacy. When I need a quick answer from a colleague, I'll message them rather than compose a formal email, and that's increasingly the norm. Where email retains its foothold is in formal, documented correspondence — contracts, confirmations, that kind of thing — where you need a written record. So rather than conquering all communication, I suspect email will settle into a narrower, more ceremonial role.

Question 6 · TV

Has the Internet significantly altered your television viewing habits?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Enormously, and I'd say the shift was almost overnight. I can barely remember the last time I watched something at a scheduled broadcast time; I stream everything on demand, which gives me complete autonomy over what I watch and when. The side effect, of course, is that I now flick through endless catalogues and sometimes spend longer choosing a programme than actually watching it. In that sense, the Internet hasn't just altered my viewing habits — it has fundamentally rewired my relationship with television itself.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe a useful electronic device you would like to own.
You should say:
  • what it is
  • how it would help your life
  • if it would be expensive to buy
  • whether it would be difficult to learn
  • and explain why you would like it
Band 9 · Model Answer

The device I'd most like to own is a pair of premium noise-cancelling headphones. It might sound mundane, but for someone who works in shared spaces and spends a fair amount of time commuting, it would genuinely transform my daily life.

The headphones I have in mind are from one of the leading audio brands — they're wireless, boast industry-leading active noise cancellation, and are renowned for their exceptional sound quality. What appeals to me most is the way they create a cocoon of silence: the moment you switch on the cancellation, the ambient chatter of a café or the rumble of a train simply melts away. That would help me enormously, because I struggle to concentrate amid background noise, and I'd be able to work effectively anywhere, whether that's on a packed carriage or in a bustling airport lounge.

In terms of cost, they're undeniably pricey — you're looking at several hundred pounds — which is exactly why I've hesitated to buy them. I keep telling myself it's a justifiable investment, since I'd use them daily for years, and when you spread that cost across the lifespan of the product, the price becomes far more palatable.

As for whether they'd be difficult to learn, not at all. You pair them with your phone in seconds, and the controls are intuitive — taps and swipes on the ear cup, nothing more. Honestly, the hardest part would be resisting the urge to wear them around the house all day.

So, in a nutshell, it's a simple, practical device that would quietly revolutionise both my focus and my listening pleasure — and one day soon, I'll probably just take the plunge and buy them.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

What is the best invention in the past hundred years?

Band 9 · Model Answer

That's a marvellous question, and I suspect most people would nominate the Internet, but I'd like to make a case for antibiotics. They transformed medicine almost overnight, turning infections that were once death sentences into minor inconveniences, and they underpin virtually everything in modern surgery and cancer treatment. That said, the Internet deserves enormous credit too — it has democratised knowledge on a scale that would have been unimaginable to previous generations. If I had to choose one, I'd lean toward antibiotics, purely because they've saved more lives than any other single breakthrough, and arguably they paved the way for the technological age by keeping populations healthy enough to build it.

Discussion 2

What influence does technology have on human life?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Technology exerts a pervasive influence that touches nearly every facet of existence, from the moment we check our phones on waking to the smart systems regulating our homes. On the positive side, it has revolutionised healthcare, made education accessible to millions, and shrunk the world through instant communication. But there's a shadow side: our attention spans have fragmented, we're increasingly sedentary, and there's a troubling erosion of privacy. I think the fundamental challenge is that technology has outpaced our ability to regulate it wisely — it's a remarkably powerful tool, but one we're still learning to wield with restraint.

Discussion 3

What can individuals do for scientific and technological research?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think there's a misconception that research is exclusively the domain of universities and well-funded laboratories, when in reality individuals can contribute meaningfully. Citizen science projects, for example, have mobilised ordinary people to classify galaxies, track bird populations, and monitor air quality — datasets that would be impractical for researchers to gather alone. Beyond participation, individuals can shape research by advocating for funding priorities, engaging with public consultations, and, importantly, by staying scientifically literate, because an informed public creates the political will that ultimately sustains scientific progress.

Discussion 4

Do you think it is mandatory to teach technology in schools?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'd go further than mandatory — I'd call it indispensable. We're preparing children for a world in which technological literacy is as fundamental as reading and writing, so leaving it out of the curriculum would be doing them a profound disservice. That said, I don't think it should be taught in isolation as a glorified IT class; it ought to be woven across subjects, so students learn not just how to use tools but how to think critically about them — the ethics of algorithms, the reliability of online information, that kind of thing. In short, it's not simply desirable; it's an educational necessity.
