Health & Wellbeing

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Exercise

Do you do much exercise?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I do, though I'd stop short of calling myself a fitness enthusiast. I aim to exercise four or five times a week — a mixture of running, swimming, and the occasional weights session — not because I'm training for anything in particular, but because I've noticed the difference it makes to my energy and mood when I don't. The key for me is variety, because monotony is the fastest way to fall out of the habit entirely.

Question 2 · Food

Do you have a healthy diet?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'd like to think so, though I'm realistic about my weaknesses. I cook most of my own meals from fresh ingredients, I'm careful with portion sizes, and I rarely touch sugary drinks, which I think puts me ahead of the curve. That said, I have a genuine weakness for pastries and the occasional takeaway, so I wouldn't claim my diet is exemplary — it's more a case of good intentions with the occasional lapse, which I've made peace with.

Question 3 · Lifestyle

Has your life changed much in the last year?

Band 9 · Model Answer

It has, more than I anticipated, and the changes have been mostly positive. I've become far more deliberate about my routines — sleep, exercise, and downtime are all scheduled rather than left to chance, which was a real shift for me. I've also started prioritising my mental wellbeing much more consciously, something I used to neglect. If anything, the past year has taught me that small, consistent adjustments matter far more than grand resolutions.

Part 1 · Questions 4–6

Question 4 · Sports

Do you play any sports?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I play a bit, though more recreationally than competitively. I enjoy football with friends at the weekend, and I've recently taken up badminton, which I find genuinely addictive — it's fast, sociable, and demands more coordination than you'd expect. What appeals to me about sports is that they combine exercise with enjoyment, so it never feels like a chore, and I think that's the real secret to staying active in the long term.

Question 5 · Daily Routine

What would you like to change about your daily routine?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The one thing I'd change is my tendency to work late into the evening, which I know is not doing me any favours. I'd like to reclaim that time for winding down properly — reading, going for an evening walk, or simply doing nothing, which I'm frankly not very good at. I've also considered getting up a little earlier, because the morning is the only quiet time I get, and I suspect that small shift would improve my whole day.

Question 6 · Food

Do you prefer eating at home or eating out?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I genuinely enjoy both, but for different reasons, so my preference depends on the circumstances. Eating out appeals when I want to relax and be social without any of the preparation or washing up, and a good restaurant feels like a treat rather than a routine. Eating at home, though, gives me control over ingredients and portions, and there's a quiet satisfaction in making a good meal from scratch. If pressed, I'd say home cooking wins on balance, but I'd miss the indulgence of eating out.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe something healthy you enjoy doing.
  • what you do
  • where you do it
  • who you do it with
  • how long you have been doing it
  • and explain why you think doing this is healthy
Band 9 · Model Answer

The healthy thing I enjoy doing, and I can genuinely say I look forward to it, is swimming. It's not something I took up for health reasons initially, but it has become a cornerstone of my wellbeing.

What I do is fairly simple: I swim two or three times a week, usually in the early morning when the pool is quiet. I tend to swim a mix of lengths — some steady freestyle to build endurance, and some faster intervals to keep things interesting. It usually takes me about forty-five minutes, and I've come to value that time enormously, because it's one of the few activities where I'm completely disconnected from my phone and my inbox.

I do it at the municipal pool, which is about fifteen minutes from my home. It's a proper competition-size pool, and I've developed a real fondness for the atmosphere there — the echo of the water, the discipline of the lane ropes, the way the morning light comes through the high windows. I usually swim alone, which suits me, though occasionally a friend joins me, and we'll turn it into a social occasion with breakfast afterwards.

I've been swimming regularly for about six years now, ever since a friend persuaded me to join her at the pool one January. I'll be honest — the first few weeks were a struggle, and I genuinely didn't enjoy it. But something clicked around the three-month mark, when I realised I was sleeping better and handling pressure more calmly. Since then it has become a habit I protect jealously, and I can tell almost immediately when I've missed it for a week.

The reason I think it's healthy goes well beyond the physical. Obviously it's excellent exercise — it works nearly every muscle group with minimal impact on the joints, and it's superb for cardiovascular fitness. But the mental benefits are just as significant. There's a meditative quality to swimming: the rhythm of the strokes, the control of the breathing, and the solitude combine to quiet my mind in a way that little else achieves. I always leave the pool calmer and clearer-headed, and I'm convinced that consistent swimming has genuinely improved both my fitness and my resilience.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

How can people improve their health?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think the fundamental principle is that prevention beats cure — most of the chronic conditions that dominate modern healthcare, from heart disease to type 2 diabetes, are heavily influenced by lifestyle, which means they're substantially avoidable. On a practical level, that translates into the unglamorous basics: regular movement, a balanced diet, adequate sleep, and managing stress, none of which are particularly exciting but all of which compound over time. The deeper challenge is that these habits require consistency, and consistency is precisely what most people struggle to sustain, which is why I'd argue habit-building deserves as much attention as medical advice itself.

Discussion 2

Do elderly people exercise much in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

It's a mixed picture, honestly. On the one hand, you see plenty of older people who are remarkably active — walking groups, morning tai chi in the parks, and so on — and that's very much part of the culture. On the other hand, the generation currently reaching retirement grew up in a more sedentary era, and many are now dealing with the consequences. What's encouraging is that awareness is growing, and there's a real push to encourage activity in later life, because the evidence that it preserves independence is genuinely compelling.

Discussion 3

Do you think all illnesses can be prevented?

Band 9 · Model Answer

No, I don't think all illnesses can be prevented, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who claimed otherwise. Lifestyle clearly plays a huge role in many chronic conditions, and prevention is powerful, but there's a stubbornly large category of illness — genetic conditions, many cancers, autoimmune disorders — that simply isn't preventable through behaviour alone. What worries me about the prevention narrative is that it can quietly blame people for illnesses they never had a realistic chance of avoiding. So I'd say prevention is essential but far from sufficient; it reduces risk, it doesn't eliminate it.

Discussion 4

Do you think healthcare should be free?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I believe healthcare should be universally accessible, though the question of whether it should be entirely free is more nuanced than it first appears. Nobody should be denied treatment because they can't afford it, and I think that principle is non-negotiable in a civilised society. That said, genuinely free systems face real pressures — demand is effectively unlimited, which leads to queues, rationing, and deferred care. My ideal would be a system that guarantees essential care to everyone while allowing people to supplement it privately, because I think that combination balances compassion with sustainability.
