Environment & Climate

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Weather

What is the climate like in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The climate in my country is genuinely temperate, with four distinct seasons, although the weather has become noticeably more erratic in recent years. We get crisp, cold winters, a pleasant spring, warm summers that occasionally tip into heatwaves, and a long, golden autumn. What I find striking is how much the seasons have started to blur — winters feel milder than they once did, and I think most people would attribute that to broader climate patterns rather than simple annual variation.

Question 2 · Seasons

What is your favourite season?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Without hesitation, autumn — or fall, as some people call it. There's something almost magical about the way the landscape turns amber and copper, and the air has that crisp, clean quality that makes a walk genuinely invigorating. I also love that it's a season of transition: the intense summer heat has subsided, but the harshness of winter hasn't arrived, so it's the perfect time for outdoor activities. It's the season when I feel most energised, both physically and creatively.

Question 3 · Environment

What are some environmental problems in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

We face a familiar set of environmental challenges, though I'd say the most visible one is air pollution in the larger cities, largely driven by traffic and industrial emissions. Alongside that, there's a persistent problem with plastic waste, particularly single-use packaging, and our rivers have suffered from industrial runoff for decades. What's encouraging is that these issues are finally getting the attention they deserve — recycling schemes have expanded, and there's real momentum behind renewable energy, so I'm cautiously optimistic that the trajectory is shifting.

Question 4 · Trees

Do you think there are enough trees in most cities?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Not really, and I think it's a fair criticism of modern urban planning. In the city centres you typically find ornamental trees lining the boulevards, but vast swathes of residential and commercial areas are dominated by concrete, with precious little greenery to soften them. Trees do far more than look pleasant — they absorb carbon, cool the streets in summer, and provide habitats for birds and insects. So I'd welcome a serious push toward urban forestry, not as a cosmetic afterthought, but as a core part of how we design cities.

Question 5 · Animals

What types of animals are kept as pets in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Cats and dogs dominate by a wide margin — they're almost a cultural institution in my country. Beyond that, you see a growing number of people keeping smaller pets, particularly rabbits, hamsters, and increasingly exotic species like reptiles, though I have mixed feelings about that trend, since many of those animals have very specific welfare needs. What heartens me is the parallel growth in adoption culture; shelters are far busier than they used to be, and people are increasingly choosing to rescue rather than buy.

Question 6 · Outdoor Activities

Do you like outdoor activities?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I love them, and I'd say they're an essential counterweight to my largely desk-bound life. Whether it's hiking in the hills, cycling along the river, or simply a long walk in the park, being outdoors has a remarkable effect on my mood — it's almost as if the fresh air resets my mind. I particularly value activities that get me away from screens altogether, because that's when I feel the mental clutter finally begin to clear.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe an environmental problem or event.
  • what it is
  • where it is happening
  • what problems it causes
  • what you think should be done about it
  • and explain what you think will happen in the future
Band 9 · Model Answer

The environmental problem I'd like to talk about is the pollution of the river that runs through my hometown. It's not a dramatic, headline-grabbing disaster, but it's a persistent and damaging issue that has shaped the area for decades.

What's happening is that the river, which once supported fishing and swimming, has become badly polluted through a combination of industrial discharge upstream and untreated wastewater during heavy rains. The visible consequences are hard to ignore: the water frequently takes on an unhealthy tinge, algae blooms appear in summer, and there's a constant litter problem along the banks. The less visible consequences are arguably worse — the ecosystem has suffered significantly, fish populations have collapsed, and the biodiversity that once thrived there has diminished to a fraction of what it was.

The problems this causes ripple outward into the community. People no longer use the river for recreation, property values near the water have suffered, and there's a lingering sense among residents that the authorities have simply accepted the pollution as the price of industrial activity. There's also a public health dimension, because contaminated water isn't only an environmental issue — it poses risks to anyone who comes into contact with it.

In terms of what should happen next, I think it comes down to three things. First, the authorities need to enforce the regulations that already exist, because a lot of the discharge happens simply because penalties are cheaper than compliance. Second, there has to be genuine investment in treatment infrastructure, not just promises. And third, local communities need a real say in how the river is managed, since they are the ones who live with the consequences. If those three things line up, I think recovery is genuinely possible.

Looking ahead, I'm cautiously optimistic, and I can see a plausible path toward improvement. There's been growing public pressure, and local campaigns have succeeded in forcing the issue onto the political agenda. New treatment infrastructure is being planned, and there are encouraging examples of similar rivers elsewhere that have been restored to health within a generation. My hope is that my hometown's river follows that same trajectory — I think it will take sustained commitment and genuine investment, but I genuinely believe the situation can be turned around within my lifetime.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

Why should people be concerned about the environment?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Because the stakes could hardly be higher — the environment isn't a peripheral concern, it's the system that sustains every aspect of our lives, from the air we breathe to the food we eat. What worries me is that environmental damage tends to be cumulative and invisible: you don't notice a declining ecosystem until it collapses, by which point the damage is often irreversible. There's also a question of fairness, because the communities that suffer most from pollution and climate disruption are frequently those that contributed least to it. So concern isn't a luxury; it's a rational response to genuine risk.

Discussion 2

Is water pollution a problem in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

It's a real concern, though the picture varies enormously between regions. In the cities, ageing infrastructure means wastewater occasionally seeps into waterways, and agricultural runoff — fertilisers and pesticides — remains a stubborn problem in rural areas. On the brighter side, there's been a visible improvement over the past couple of decades, with stricter regulations on industrial discharge and a growing number of river restoration projects. So while it's far from solved, I'd say the direction of travel is genuinely positive.

Discussion 3

How can people protect the environment?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think the most powerful shifts start at home, precisely because they compound over time. Things like cutting down on single-use plastics, choosing public transport over driving, and being conscious of energy use might seem trivial in isolation, but multiplied across millions of households they become significant. Beyond personal habits, there's also real power in collective action — supporting conservation organisations, choosing sustainable brands, and voting for policies that prioritise the environment. The key insight is that individual and systemic change reinforce each other; neither is effective on its own.

Discussion 4

Do you think problems with water cleanliness will improve in the future?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'm cautiously hopeful, though I'd stop short of confident. On the one hand, water treatment technology has advanced dramatically, and public awareness is higher than it's ever been, which creates genuine political pressure for cleaner rivers and lakes. On the other hand, the pace of change is glacial, and new threats keep emerging — microplastics, for instance, were barely on the radar a decade ago. So I'd say the trend is upward, but whether it moves fast enough to outpace the damage is still very much an open question.
