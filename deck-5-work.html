Work & Employment

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Work

Do you work or are you a student?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'm currently working, though I've also studied in the fairly recent past, so I feel I occupy a middle ground. My job keeps me busy and intellectually engaged, but I've retained a student's mindset — I still take courses, read widely, and try to learn something new each week. I suppose the honest answer is that the boundary between the two has blurred for me, because in my field, learning never really stops, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who claimed otherwise.

Question 2 · Daily Routine

Is your daily routine different at the weekend?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Yes, considerably, though perhaps not in the way people expect. During the week my life runs on rails — the alarm, the commute, the schedule — whereas at the weekend I deliberately let the structure dissolve: later starts, longer meals, and time with friends that isn't governed by the clock. The contrast is important to me, because I think the weekend only works as a genuine reset if it's meaningfully different from the working week, and mine emphatically is.

Question 3 · Work

Are there good work opportunities in your home country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'd say the opportunities are decent, though they're unevenly distributed across sectors and regions. The major cities offer strong prospects, particularly in technology, finance, and professional services, and there's a healthy appetite for skilled workers. Outside those hubs, though, the picture is patchier, and I know plenty of talented people who've had to relocate to find work that matches their qualifications. So I'd describe it as a market of genuine opportunity, but one that rewards mobility and flexibility.

Part 1 · Questions 4–6

Question 4 · Major

Do you plan to use what you studied in your future career?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Absolutely, and I'd say it shapes my work every day, though often in indirect ways. My studies taught me how to think rigorously, how to research, and how to communicate complex ideas clearly, and those transferable skills are far more valuable than any specific facts I memorised. That said, I've also had to learn a great deal on the job that no classroom could have prepared me for, which I think is true of most careers. So my education gave me the foundation, but experience has built the house.

Question 5 · Timing

Is being late acceptable in your culture?

Band 9 · Model Answer

It's tolerated in some contexts and frowned upon in others, which makes it a genuinely subtle cultural signal. Among friends, lateness is often forgiven with a shrug — the atmosphere is relaxed and no one keeps a strict ledger. But in professional settings, punctuality is taken seriously, and chronic lateness is widely read as a lack of respect or organisation. So the cultural rule, as I understand it, is that your lateness should never inconvenience anyone else, and that principle guides most people's behaviour.

Question 6 · Business

Would you prefer to work for a company or be self-employed?

Band 9 · Model Answer

That's a genuine dilemma for me, because both paths have real appeal. Working for a company offers stability, structure, and the simple pleasure of being part of a team, and I think I'd learn a great deal from experienced colleagues. Being self-employed, though, offers autonomy and the chance to build something of my own, which is seductive in a different way. If I'm honest, I suspect I'll do both at different stages of my career — the security of employment early on, and the freedom of self-employment once I've built the skills and confidence.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe your dream job.
  • what kind of work you would do
  • what the working conditions would be like
  • what kind of workplace you would work in
  • how it would fit around the rest of your life
  • and explain if you think you will be able to get a job like that
Band 9 · Model Answer

My dream job, if I'm honest about it, would combine meaningful work with genuine autonomy, and I think I can describe it quite specifically.

The kind of work I'd love to do is work that involves solving problems and helping people — I'm drawn to roles where I can see a tangible connection between my effort and someone else's improvement. In practical terms, that could mean a senior role in education, consultancy, or even a specialist technical field, but the common thread is that the work would have a clear purpose beyond simply generating profit. I want to look back at my career and point to things I changed for the better.

The working conditions would matter to me. I'd want genuine flexibility — the freedom to manage my own hours and, to a large degree, my own priorities, with the trust that comes from being judged on outcomes rather than hours at a desk. I'd also want the pace to be sustainable, one that doesn't burn people out, where long hours are the exception rather than the expectation.

As for the workplace itself, I picture something modern and collaborative — an environment designed for focus and for genuine interaction, with spaces for deep work alongside spaces where ideas can be exchanged informally. Crucially, I'd want to be surrounded by colleagues who are both talented and humane, because the people you work with determine the quality of the experience far more than the building or the brand.

Crucially, I'd want this job to fit around the rest of my life rather than consume it. I've seen too many people define themselves entirely by their work, and I think that's a mistake. So I'd want boundaries — reasonable hours, the freedom to be present for my family, and holidays that I actually take. To me, a dream job isn't just about the work itself; it's about the life it makes possible. If the job costs you the people around you, it stops being a dream job, however impressive it looks on paper.

Do I think I'll actually get a job like that? I'm optimistic, though I'm realistic that it won't arrive fully formed. I suspect I'll have to build toward it — accumulating experience, developing the right reputation, and making deliberate choices at each career crossroads. But the fact that I can picture it so clearly is, I think, the first step toward making it happen.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

Do you think job satisfaction is more important than salary?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think the premise is slightly misleading, because the two aren't really competing values — it's more that their relative weight shifts over time. Early in a career, salary often matters enormously, because it funds stability and independence, and there's no shame in that. But there's abundant evidence that once basic needs are met, satisfaction, autonomy, and a sense of purpose increasingly determine whether people thrive or burn out. The deeper point is that a job you despise will eventually drain you regardless of the pay, so I'd argue satisfaction isn't a luxury — it's a sustainability requirement.

Discussion 2

What skills are needed to get a good job these days?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The fundamentals haven't changed as much as people think: communication, reliability, and the ability to solve problems are still the core currency of employment. What's shifted is the context — digital literacy is now assumed rather than prized, and there's far more emphasis on adaptability, because the half-life of specific technical skills keeps shrinking. I'd also highlight emotional intelligence, which sounds soft but is often the difference between a good employee and an indispensable one. In short, the skills that endure are the ones that machines can't replicate.

Discussion 3

What is the difference between white-collar and blue-collar jobs?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The traditional distinction was a matter of what you do — manual versus intellectual labour — but that line has blurred considerably as even white-collar work has become more routine and blue-collar trades have grown more technical and skilled. What remains different is largely social and economic: white-collar roles have historically enjoyed more prestige, flexibility, and upward mobility, though that gap is narrowing as the demand for skilled trades pushes wages up. I think the most honest characterisation is that the old hierarchy is crumbling, and the real divide now runs between work that requires constant learning and work that doesn't.

Discussion 4

What jobs do you think are most valuable to society?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'd resist the temptation to rank professions, because value to society is genuinely multidimensional, but I can say which ones I'd defend most fiercely. Teachers, nurses, and care workers form the quiet infrastructure of a functioning society, and their value is only fully appreciated when they're absent. I'd also argue that people who maintain essential services — water, energy, transport — are indispensable in ways that are easy to overlook. The uncomfortable truth is that our pay and prestige systems don't reliably reflect social value, and correcting that imbalance would, I think, make society healthier in the broadest sense.
