IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

Question 1 · Travel

Where was the last place you visited on holiday?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The last place I visited was a coastal town about three hours from where I live, and it was exactly the escape I needed. It's the kind of place that hasn't been fully overtaken by tourism — narrow streets, family-run restaurants, and a seafront that feels wonderfully unhurried. What made it memorable wasn't any single attraction, but the pace of life there; within a day I'd completely forgotten my usual preoccupations, which is surely the mark of a successful holiday.

Question 2 · Internet

How important is the Internet to you?

Band 9 · Model Answer

It's become genuinely indispensable, to the point where I'm slightly uncomfortable admitting it. I use it for work, for staying in touch with family abroad, for banking, for learning, and frankly for a large portion of my entertainment — its absence would be felt in virtually every corner of my day. I do try to maintain some perspective, because I remember life before it was ubiquitous, and I think that memory helps me treat it as a tool rather than a master.

Question 3 · Language

Do you speak any foreign languages?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I do — I speak a couple of languages at varying levels, and I wish I had more fluency than I do. My English is strong, and I have a working knowledge of a second language that I can use comfortably for travel and everyday conversation, though I still stumble when things get technical or fast-paced. What I've learned is that languages are like muscles: they atrophy without use, so my real challenge is maintaining what I've built, because the effort of upkeep never quite ends.

Question 4 · Food

Do you enjoy cooking food from other countries?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I genuinely enjoy it, though my repertoire is limited. There's something satisfying about attempting dishes from other culinary traditions — the unfamiliar spices and techniques make cooking feel like exploration rather than routine, and the results are usually more interesting than my everyday cooking. It's also a lovely way to travel vicariously, since a well-executed foreign dish carries the memory of the place it came from. My only complaint is that authenticity often demands ingredients that aren't easy to source locally.

Question 5 · Clothes

Have you ever bought clothes online from another country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I have, and it's a mixed experience that taught me a lasting lesson. The first time, the prices were excellent and the quality was indistinguishable from local shopping, which made me a convert. The second time, the item didn't match its photographs, and returning it was so laborious — international shipping, customs paperwork — that I effectively wrote off the money. So I'd say I've learned to be selective: it's worth it for well-established international brands, but a gamble for anything else.

Question 6 · Travel

Would you like to go back there again?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'd love to, and in fact I'm already planning it. There are places I barely scratched the surface of — a couple of towns inland, some hiking trails, and several museums I skipped in favour of the beach — and I have that nagging feeling of unfinished business that only a return visit can resolve. Part of the appeal is revisiting the familiar spots that made the first trip special, because places, like people, reveal different layers on a second meeting.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe a small business you want to start.
  • what business it would be
  • when you want to start it
  • why you have not started it yet
  • what the biggest challenge would be
  • and explain why you want to start this business
Band 9 · Model Answer

The small business I'd love to start is a speciality coffee roastery — a small, focused operation that sources quality beans directly and roasts them in-house, rather than the kind of cafe that simply serves someone else's product.

The business would be quite specific. I'd start with a small roasting facility — modest premises with a professional roaster — and sell through three channels: a tiny shopfront where people can buy freshly roasted beans, a wholesale arm supplying local cafes and restaurants, and a modest online store for delivery. The emphasis would be on transparency and quality: knowing the farms the coffee comes from, roasting in small batches, and being able to tell every customer exactly where their coffee began its journey.

When I'd like to start it is the complicated part, because my honest answer is that I have wanted to start it for several years, and I keep postponing it. I'd love to begin within the next couple of years, once I've built sufficient savings and, frankly, the confidence to take the risk.

The reason I haven't started it yet is a combination of caution and circumstance. Financially, it requires real capital — equipment, premises, and the ability to survive the first year, when most small businesses either break even or fail. Practically, I have commitments that make it difficult to dedicate the hours a new venture demands. And honestly, there's an element of fear: the comfortable path is easier, and leaving it for an uncertain venture takes a courage I'm still building.

The biggest challenge, I think, would be the first two years. Coffee is a crowded market, and standing out requires either an exceptional product or an unusually strong story, and building both takes time. Cash flow is another real risk — a roastery has to buy green beans, pay for roasting, and then wait for sales to catch up, which can leave you exposed. And there's the simple challenge of consistency: a business built on quality falls apart the moment the quality slips, so the discipline would have to come from me every single day.

But the reason I want to start it is genuine and specific. I'm passionate about coffee in a way that borders on obsession, and I've been struck by how much of what we drink is mediocre when the raw material can be extraordinary. I want to build something that demonstrates that quality and ethics can be commercially viable — a business that treats its suppliers fairly, pays its staff properly, and still delights its customers.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

In your opinion, do business people have to work long hours?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think it's more accurate to say they work long hours in many cases, but it's neither inevitable nor universal, and the pattern is shifting. In some industries, particularly in the corporate world, long hours have become a badge of commitment, which I suspect is as much about signalling as about genuine necessity. What's changing, though, is the conversation around productivity — there's growing evidence that output doesn't scale linearly with hours, and that rested, balanced workers often outperform exhausted ones. So the short answer is yes, they often do, but I'd argue that's a cultural choice rather than a law of economics.

Discussion 2

How can a small business grow big?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The classic path involves finding a genuine niche and then compounding small advantages — a distinctive product, exceptional service, or a market the giants have overlooked. From there, growth tends to come from repeat customers and word of mouth, which is the most trustworthy marketing there is, and from reinvesting rather than extracting profits too early. I'd also emphasise that many successful businesses grew through partnerships and distribution deals rather than pure organic growth. Above all, I think resilience matters most: most growing businesses survive a succession of near-failures, and persistence is what separates those that scale from those that stall.

Discussion 3

What are the impacts of globalisation on small and large businesses?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The impact has been profoundly asymmetrical. Large businesses have generally thrived under globalisation — they have the resources to navigate global supply chains, exploit cost differences, and enter new markets at scale, and in many cases they've become genuinely multinational in outlook. Small businesses, by contrast, face a double squeeze: they compete with imported goods on price while their local advantage erodes. That said, globalisation has also handed small businesses unprecedented tools — e-commerce platforms and digital marketing let a two-person company sell worldwide, which was unthinkable a generation ago. So the honest summary is that globalisation has raised the stakes for everyone, rewarding agility and punishing complacency at every scale.

Discussion 4

What skills are required to start a small business?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'd start with a handful of skills that are both practical and non-obvious. Financial literacy is first — most small businesses fail not from lack of customers but from mismanaged cash flow, and understanding margins, runway, and break-even points is survival-level knowledge. Beyond that, sales and negotiation matter more than most people expect, because every aspect of a business, from suppliers to staff, is ultimately a negotiation. And I'd add genuine customer empathy — the ability to see your product through the buyer's eyes rather than your own. Everything else can be learned or hired; those three are foundational.
