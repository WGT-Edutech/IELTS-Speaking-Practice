Education & Learning

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Major

Why did you choose your major or field of study?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I chose my field largely out of genuine curiosity rather than any strategic calculation. I've always been fascinated by how things work beneath the surface, and my subject allows me to explore exactly that, which made the decision feel almost inevitable. That said, I was also mindful of the practical side — I wanted a qualification that would keep doors open rather than narrow my options. In hindsight, it was the rare case where passion and pragmatism happened to point in the same direction.

Question 2 · Books

What kind of books do you like to read?

Band 9 · Model Answer

My reading tastes are fairly eclectic, but I'm drawn primarily to non-fiction — history, psychology, and the occasional well-researched biography. There's something deeply satisfying about a book that leaves you with a genuinely new way of seeing the world. I do dip into fiction as well, particularly literary novels with strong characterisation, though I'm somewhat selective, because with a busy schedule I'd rather read fewer books properly than skim dozens superficially.

Question 3 · High School

Do you still keep in touch with friends from high school?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I do, though the circle has naturally tightened over the years. A handful of my closest school friends remain in regular contact, and we make a point of meeting up whenever our schedules align — there's an ease to those friendships that newer ones rarely replicate, because you share a whole history. Interestingly, the friendships that have endured aren't necessarily the ones I expected to last; it's the people who've grown in similar directions who've stayed in my life, which I suppose reflects how much we all evolve.

Question 4 · Teachers

Who was your favourite teacher in high school?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Without doubt, my history teacher. He had this remarkable gift for making the past feel urgent and alive — his lessons were less lectures than stories, and he treated us like young intellectuals rather than children. What I remember most, though, was his patience: he would stay after class to debate points with anyone who was curious, and he never made you feel foolish for asking a naive question. He's the reason I still approach learning with genuine enthusiasm rather than obligation.

Question 5 · Study

Do you work or are you a student?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'm currently working, though I've also studied in the fairly recent past, so I feel I occupy a middle ground. My job keeps me busy and intellectually engaged, but I've retained a student's mindset — I still take courses, read widely, and try to learn something new each week. I suppose the honest answer is that the boundary between the two has blurred for me, because in my field, learning never really stops, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who claimed otherwise.

Question 6 · Books

Do you think electronic books are better than paper books?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think it depends entirely on what you're reading and why. For reference material, e-books are arguably unbeatable — searchable, portable, and instantly accessible, which makes them ideal for study. But for immersive reading, I still believe the physical book has a subtle advantage: there's something about the weight of the paper and the progress of the page that deepens concentration. So I'd frame it not as a contest between better and worse, but as two tools suited to different purposes.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe a school you attended or are attending.
  • what it looks like from the outside
  • what it looks like inside
  • what facilities it has
  • how the school made you feel
  • and explain whether it is a good school building
Band 9 · Model Answer

The school I'd like to describe is the secondary school I attended, and it occupies a surprisingly vivid place in my memory.

From the outside, it was a fairly imposing building — a classic brick structure from the early twentieth century, with tall windows, a grand central entrance, and a clock tower that you could see from several streets away. It looked exactly like the kind of school you see in films: solid, slightly austere, and full of history. I remember feeling a little intimidated walking through those gates on the first day, though that feeling didn't last long.

Inside, the impression was quite different. The corridors were wide and flooded with natural light, and the walls were covered with student artwork and displays, which gave the place a warmth that the exterior didn't promise. The classrooms were spacious and well-equipped, and there was a genuine atmosphere of purpose about the building — you could tell it had been designed at a time when education was taken very seriously.

In terms of facilities, it was well ahead of the average for its time. We had a proper library with an extensive collection, science laboratories that were actually used for experiments rather than just admired, a large sports hall, and music rooms where I spent a great deal of my free time. There was even a small theatre, which I still remember with affection because our school productions were the highlight of the year.

How the school made me feel is perhaps the most important thing about it. It gave me a genuine sense of belonging — the kind of place where you felt known rather than processed. There was a quiet pride in the building itself, and the teachers clearly shared it, which rubbed off on the students. I remember feeling that what we were doing mattered, and that the school was somewhere I wanted to be rather than somewhere I had to be. That, I think, is what turns a building into something more than just a building.

Would I call it a good school building? Yes, emphatically. It had character, it was maintained with evident pride, and it created an environment that made learning feel substantial. In an age where schools are increasingly built to a functional template, I feel fortunate to have spent my formative years in a building with that much soul.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

How are education priorities today different from those in the past?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The most striking shift is the move from memorisation toward skills — education today is far more concerned with critical thinking, collaboration, and digital literacy than with the sheer accumulation of facts, which made sense when knowledge was scarce. There's also been a democratising trend: online learning has cracked open access in a way that was unthinkable a generation ago. That said, I worry the pendulum has swung too far in places, and that some timeless academic virtues — sustained concentration, deep reading, the discipline of rigorous argument — are being quietly undervalued.

Discussion 2

What is your opinion on the way languages are taught in schools?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Honestly, I think language teaching has improved enormously, but it still lags behind what the research suggests works best. Too many classrooms remain anchored in grammar drills and translation, when we know that immersive, communicative practice is far more effective — you acquire a language by using it, not by analysing it. That said, I've seen genuinely inspiring exceptions, particularly where teachers create real communicative pressure and treat mistakes as part of the process rather than failures. The gap between best practice and common practice is closing, but it's closing slower than it should.

Discussion 3

How can the type of school you attend affect career success?

Band 9 · Model Answer

It can have a profound influence, though rarely in the direct way people assume. Prestigious institutions confer an immediate advantage through networks and signalling, but I think the deeper effect runs through expectations and peer culture — the people around you shape your ambition and your standards. That said, school type is far from destiny: determination, curiosity, and the ability to learn independently can compensate for a great deal. The honest conclusion is that schools open doors, but they don't decide who walks through them.

Discussion 4

What changes do you think will happen in classrooms in the near future?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I suspect classrooms will become far more personalised and technology-mediated within a decade or two. Adaptive learning software already tailors exercises to individual students, and I'd expect that to deepen, with AI handling routine instruction while teachers focus on mentoring, discussion, and the human dimensions of education. I also anticipate a move away from fixed timetables toward more project-based, self-paced learning, which suits the way many students actually work best. The risk, of course, is that we chase novelty at the expense of proven methods, so I hope the change is evolutionary rather than revolutionary.
