Food & Cooking

IELTS Speaking Practice · B2 Level

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

⏱ 4:30
Question 1 · Cooking

Do you enjoy cooking?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I do, though I came to it relatively late and I'm still learning. There's something genuinely satisfying about cooking — the combination of technique, timing, and taste means no two attempts are ever quite the same, and a well-executed dish carries a quiet sense of achievement. I also find it meditative in a strange way: the focus required to chop, season, and time everything properly pushes aside the noise of the day. My only regret is that I didn't start earlier, because I'd have saved a fortune and eaten better.

Question 2 · Cooking

What types of things can you cook?

Band 9 · Model Answer

My repertoire is practical rather than adventurous — I'd describe myself as a solid home cook with a small set of reliable dishes. I can handle the classics: pasta dishes, curries, soups, roasted vegetables, and a few recipes passed down from my family that I guard jealously. What I've noticed is that my cooking improved dramatically once I stopped following recipes slavishly and started understanding principles — why things brown, why sauces split, why seasoning matters. Since then, I've been comfortable improvising, which is where the real fun lies.

Question 3 · National Food

What kinds of food are popular in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The food scene is wonderfully diverse, but a few things define it. We have a strong tradition of hearty, home-cooked food built on fresh, seasonal ingredients, and there are regional specialities that locals defend with surprising passion. Alongside the traditional, the cities have embraced global cuisine enthusiastically, and I'd say the younger generation is genuinely adventurous — more likely to try a new cuisine than their parents were. The unifying theme, I think, is that food is central to social life here; meals are rarely just fuel.

Question 4 · Diet

Do you have a healthy diet?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I'd say it's broadly healthy, though not fanatically so, and I think that moderation is itself part of a healthy approach. I cook most of my meals from scratch, eat plenty of vegetables, and try to keep processed food to a minimum, which I think puts me ahead of the average. That said, I have my weaknesses — pastry, cheese, and the occasional takeaway when I'm tired — and I've made peace with them, because I've learned that extreme regimes are rarely sustainable. My philosophy is simple: good food most of the time, with room for indulgence.

Question 5 · Eating Habits

Do you prefer to eat with other people or on your own?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I prefer eating with other people, without much hesitation. A meal shared is a different experience entirely — the conversation, the pace, the sense of occasion all change, and I find food genuinely tastes better in company. That said, I've also come to appreciate eating alone, particularly at lunch, because it's one of the few moments of genuine solitude in a busy day, and there's a meditative quality to a meal eaten without distraction. So my honest answer is that both have their place, but if forced to choose, the company wins.

Question 6 · Culture

Is it an important part of your culture to have dinner parties?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Yes, it's a deeply embedded tradition, and I'd argue it's one of the cultural pillars of hospitality here. Dinner parties are how we celebrate, how we reciprocate favours, and how we deepen friendships, and there's a real social weight attached to hosting well. What's interesting is that the tradition has adapted — the formal multi-course affair has relaxed into something more casual, but the core impulse, gathering people around a table, remains entirely intact. I suspect it will persist, because it serves a human need that no digital substitute has managed to replicate.

Part 2 · Cue Card

⏱ 2:00
Describe a traditional dish from your country.
  • what it looks like
  • what it tastes like
  • when and how it is usually eaten
  • where you first tried it
  • and explain why it is popular
Band 9 · Model Answer

The traditional dish I'd like to describe is one that occupies a special place in my country's culinary identity, and it's the dish my family serves whenever there's something to celebrate.

What it looks like is, frankly, not particularly glamorous — and I think that's part of its charm. It's a substantial, slow-cooked stew, rich and deeply coloured, with tender pieces of meat that have cooked until they're almost falling apart, surrounded by vegetables and a sauce that has thickened and darkened over hours of gentle simmering. It's served in a deep bowl, usually with bread or a soft dumpling to soak up the sauce, and it looks exactly like what it is: food made with patience.

What it tastes like is difficult to describe without sounding like a restaurant review, but I'll try. The flavour is deep and savoury, built in layers — there's a richness from the slow-cooked meat, a sweetness from the vegetables that have caramelised into the sauce, and a subtle warmth from spices that perfume rather than dominate. The texture matters as much as the taste: the meat should be so tender that it yields without resistance, and the sauce should coat the back of a spoon. It's the kind of food that fills the house with a smell you can't mistake.

When and how it's eaten is almost as important as the dish itself. It's traditionally a weekend and celebration dish, because it demands time — it's not something you can produce on a rushed weekday evening. It's eaten slowly, with family, often as the centrepiece of a long Sunday lunch, and it's the kind of meal where seconds are expected and silence at the table is a sign of appreciation rather than awkwardness.

The first time I tried it was at my grandmother's house, at a family gathering when I was quite young. She cooked it herself, in enormous quantities, and the whole house smelled of it for days. I remember being allowed to help — admittedly with very small tasks, like picking herbs — and feeling enormously proud of that. Eating it at her table is still my strongest association with the dish; even now, if I order it at a restaurant, I inevitably compare it to hers, and it almost always measures up.

And why it's popular? I think because it embodies the values we associate with good food and good company: generosity, patience, and tradition. It's a dish that connects people to their grandmothers' kitchens, and in a world of convenience, food that demands time has become, paradoxically, a luxury. It's comfort, history, and hospitality all served in one bowl.

Part 3 · Discussion

⏱ 4:30
Discussion 1

In your country, is it important to have a meal together with your family?

Band 9 · Model Answer

Yes, it's genuinely important, and I'd go further: it's one of the mechanisms that hold families together. In my culture, the shared meal is where news is exchanged, decisions are discussed, and relationships are maintained, and I think its absence is one of the quieter casualties of modern life. What I find striking is that even busy families make the effort at least at weekends, which suggests the tradition is recognised as valuable rather than merely habitual. I'd argue that protecting that ritual is a small investment with outsized returns in family cohesion.

Discussion 2

What kinds of foreign food are popular in your country?

Band 9 · Model Answer

The range is impressive, and it's grown considerably in my lifetime. Asian cuisines are probably the most established — the curry houses and noodle bars have been staples for decades — but you now find excellent food from virtually everywhere, with newer waves bringing Latin American and Middle Eastern restaurants to prominence. What I find heartening is that the quality has risen alongside the variety; early adoptions of foreign food were often sanitised for local palates, whereas today's restaurants increasingly pursue authenticity. I think that reflects a genuinely cosmopolitan shift in taste.

Discussion 3

Is food now better than in the past?

Band 9 · Model Answer

That's a question with a more complicated answer than it appears, because it depends what you mean by better. In terms of choice, safety, and convenience, food is almost incomparably better — the variety available, the standards of hygiene, and the ease of access to ingredients from around the world are all unprecedented. In terms of nutritional quality, the picture is darker: the proliferation of highly processed food has created health problems that previous generations barely knew. And in terms of flavour, I'd argue it's a draw — industrial production has made some foods blander, but global trade has put genuinely good ingredients within reach. So: better in every way except the ones that matter most for health.

Discussion 4

Why are more and more people unwilling to cook nowadays?

Band 9 · Model Answer

I think the reasons are a mixture of economics and culture, and they reinforce each other. Time is the fundamental constraint — cooking well takes time that many people genuinely don't have, and the opportunity cost of an hour in the kitchen versus an affordable takeaway has shifted. There's also a skills dimension: cooking competence is no longer reliably passed down, so many people avoid it out of insecurity rather than disinterest. And the market has adapted accordingly, making cheap, convenient food more accessible than ever. The consequence is a paradox — we have more culinary resources than any previous generation, yet home cooking is in decline.
